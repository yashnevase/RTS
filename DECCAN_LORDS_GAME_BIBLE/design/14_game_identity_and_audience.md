# 14 — Product identity, audience and market hypotheses

**Status: DISCUSSION / NOT LOCKED**

## What recent discussion suggests

The user increasingly sees the game as closer to the settlement-building and political sandbox experiences of Anno 1800, Manor Lords and Mount & Blade: Bannerlord than to a pure short-match AoE-style RTS. AoE and Rise of Nations remain relevant for readable armies, resource-driven expansion and satisfying tactical action. Stronghold contributes forts and siege defense; Clash of Clans is an observation about immediate clarity and visible progression, not a commitment to mobile/free-to-play design.

Working description for discussion: **a real-time kingdom/settlement sandbox with political simulation, strategic movement, logistics and tactical battles**. This is not locked marketing terminology.

## Audience promise to investigate

- A newcomer can understand the next useful action quickly without needing to understand every system.
- Experienced players can discover deeper economic, military and political interactions over long sessions.
- Peaceful development should remain enjoyable; conflict should arise from meaningful world conditions rather than constant forced interruptions.
- Battles and military choices should be legible and satisfying.
- CPU lords should have understandable motives but not always predictable outcomes.
- Accessibility must not mean shallow systems; complexity should be layered and presented progressively.

## Product tension: long campaign vs quick competitive match

A long economic/political campaign and short skill-based competitive matches reward different pacing, progression, balance and onboarding. Do not assume both belong in the first release or that they can use identical rules. Options to discuss later include: a single-player sandbox first; a separate skirmish ruleset later; cooperative campaign; competitive matches; or a smaller set of these. The current priority remains a compelling single-player CPU experience, while multiplayer stays a future ambition.

## Market reality

- A coherent, polished game with a strong identity and visible development process has a better chance of finding an audience than a long feature list, but sales cannot be predicted from the premise or AI tooling alone.
- Stardew Valley is an exceptional success, not a realistic baseline or forecast for a first solo game. Use it as inspiration for identity, care, iteration and long-term player trust—not as a sales estimate.
- Steam and Epic are distribution channels, not audience guarantees. Plan for playtesting, a demo/vertical slice, storefront positioning, wishlists/community-building, clear screenshots/trailer, onboarding and support. Evaluate each store's current requirements and fees near release.
- Aestra: The Last Wish on Tesana is useful as an example of visible iteration and player feedback: its page displays successive versions and recent content updates. Its displayed player figure should not be assumed to mean concurrent users or paid sales, and the page does not establish the separate Unity/Opus/Tripo workflow anecdote as its production pipeline.

## Major scope decision: India-wide aspiration vs initial active region

The user's long-term vision extends across major powers and places from Delhi toward Kanyakumari. That ambition is preserved. An entire subcontinent with detailed settlements, factions, economy, diplomacy, military logistics and authored content could become too large to deliver quickly. One candidate is to make the world model/data architecture expandable while shipping an initially focused active region, with farther powers represented more abstractly or added later. This is a trade-off to discuss, not a decision already made.

## Visual and production pipeline hypotheses

A role-based workflow—design brief, asset creation, integration, tests/playtest, polish—is useful. Each AI task needs a bounded outcome and acceptance tests. AI-generated 3D assets require human review for consistent scale/style, topology, rigs, animation, performance and licensing. Avoid generating every final asset independently without a shared art bible and reusable modular kit.

## Questions for discussion

1. What is the player's primary recurring activity: managing a settlement, making realm-level decisions, directing armies, or switching between these? Rank them.
2. What should one ordinary game session feel like: calm and self-paced, tense and reactive, or a deliberate alternation?
3. Should tactical battles be a separate real-time battlefield, happen directly on the strategic map, or remain open pending prototype testing?
4. Is a distinct competitive/skirmish mode a likely later product feature, or should we focus on one campaign experience first?
5. Which portion of the India-wide world must be playable in the first release for the game to feel authentic?
