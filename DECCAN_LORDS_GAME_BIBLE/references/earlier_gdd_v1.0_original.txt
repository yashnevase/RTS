1. ONE-SENTENCE PITCH
A 2D pixel strategy game set in the Deccan, 1600s. You're a minor lord. You grow your village, manage your people, build your army, and navigate the politics of the Sultanate. You can serve, rebel, or rise. The world reacts to your decisions through simple math that feels alive. Battles are tactical. Castles are customizable. And if you play well, you become someone — maybe even a king.

2. THE DESIGN JOURNEY
This document is the product of a long design conversation. Understanding what was rejected is as important as understanding what was kept, because the rejected options represent traps that future contributors will be tempted to fall into again.

2.1 The Original Prototype
The project began as a wave-defense RTS in the style of Stronghold-lite. Working code exists:

Game.js — full RTS engine: units, pathfinding, combat, structures, projectiles, waves

PathFinder.js — A* with per-team terrain costs (enemies "pay" to break walls)

render.js — depth-sorted sprite rendering, procedural pixel art

terrain.js — procedurally generated ground

App.jsx — React HUD layer

constants.js — all tunable numbers

This prototype is the foundation. The battle system, pathfinding, and rendering will be reused. The wave-defense framing will be replaced.

2.2 Pitches Considered and Rejected
Pitch	Why Rejected
Full Bannerlord sandbox	2–4 year project. Character skills, dialogue trees, dynasty system, personal combat — each is a full game. Too much.
Pure Stronghold castle sim	Too small. No political layer. Lacks the identity that makes this project unique.
Pure AoE clone	Crowded market. AoE2:DE is free and 25 years polished. No differentiation.
Pure Anno colony sim	No soul. Numbers puzzle. Also crowded (Manor Lords, Farthest Frontier, etc.).
CoC/CR mobile multiplayer	Hardest option, not easiest. Multiplayer + live service = studio-scale.
"AoE + all features"	The 10-system trap. Every game in the list cuts 3+ systems. Combining all of them = nothing ships.
ML-trained NPCs	6–12 months of work for 10% improvement over rules. Black box, undebuggable, slow.
2.3 The Winning Insight
The final design emerged from a simple realization:

Anno's structure is proven and shippable. Put the Deccan on top of it. Replace colonization with conquest-of-inhabited-land. Add Bannerlord's politics as simple numbers. Use the existing Game.js for battles.

This respects both the ambition (a living world with politics, conquest, and consequence) and the reality (a solo dev shipping in ~1 year).

3. THE FINAL DESIGN
3.1 What The Game Is
Anno's structure, Deccan's setting, AoE's combat, Bannerlord's politics.

Layer	Inspired By	What It Provides
Overworld map	Bannerlord / Total War	Move armies, manage settlements, conduct diplomacy
Town building	Anno	Buildings, production, worker roles, upgrades
Resources	Anno	5 resources, simple chains, trade
Conquest	New mechanic	Villages are inhabited — take them, earn or crush loyalty
Combat	AoE / existing Game.js	Tactical battles on a separate map
Politics	Bannerlord	Relations, reputation, alliance, betrayal
Setting	History	1600s Deccan — Mughals, Sultanates, Marathas, Europeans
3.2 What Makes It Unique
The single thing no other 2D game does:

Conquest of inhabited land. In Anno, you settle empty islands. Here, you take villages full of people who already live there, owe allegiance to someone, and will resist, accept, or betray you. That one change turns "build a colony" into "build a kingdom by winning people over or crushing them."

This is why the setting matters. The Deccan in 1660 was a patchwork of Sultanates, Mughal pressure, local Deshmukhs, hill tribes, and European trading posts. Every village had a lord. Every lord had a Sultan. Every Sultan had rivals. Taking land meant taking people.

3.3 The Player's Role
You are a minor lord. Not a named historical figure. A person with:

One village

A small garrison (~20 troops)

A name and a reputation

A sworn allegiance to a Sultan

Through play, you can become:

A general in the Sultan's army

A rebel who breaks away

A merchant prince

A bandit lord

A king

A corpse

Same systems. Different destinies. This is what makes it a life, not a campaign.

4. THE SETTING
4.1 Time and Place
Western Deccan + Konkan coast, c. 1660.

Roughly modern Maharashtra. ~200 km × 130 km.

This is the region where Shivaji historically operated — the perfect size to hand-author, small enough to feel real, large enough to hold a full game.

4.2 Historical Context
Bijapur Sultanate — Adil Shahi dynasty, controls the southern plateau. Your overlord.

Mughal Empire — Aurangzeb's forces to the north, expanding south.

Golconda Sultanate — to the east, wealthy, distant.

Portuguese — hold Chaul and coastal enclaves. Trade guns, powder, mercenaries.

Dutch — trading posts, competing with Portuguese.

British — small outpost (Bombay-like), quiet, watching.

Local Deshmukhs — minor lords, self-interested, switch allegiances.

Hill tribes — deep in the Sahyadri, neutral, paid for passage.

4.3 Why This Setting
Underused — no 2D RTS exists in this space.

Rich in conflict — six factions with different goals.

Real geography — the Sahyadri mountains, rivers, passes, ports.

Real history — Shivaji's rise is a documented arc we can borrow from without scripting.

Cheap content — history hands us names, places, events, units, and politics for free.

5.6 Rivers and Passes
Rivers (3):

Godavari — north, crossings near Nashik/Junnar

Bhima — central, crossings near Pune/Daund

Krishna — south, crossings near Karad/Satara

Rivers are chokepoints, not obstacles. Crossings are where armies meet, caravans pay tolls, bandits wait.

Passes (4):

Northern (Torna/Junnar) — Mughal route

Central (Sinhagad) — main coast-to-plateau road

Southern (Pratapgad) — Satara to coast

Raigad approach — coastal route, safer, slower

Whoever holds the passes holds the map.

5.7 The Decisions the Map Creates
Where do you start? Pune (safe, poor), Junnar (rich, exposed), Chaul (wealthy, foreign).

Which fort do you take first? Torna (easy), Sinhagad (hard, impregnable), Rajgad (classic).

Pass or town? Pass is defensible, earns nothing. Town earns, is exposed.

Coast or plateau? Coast has trade, guns, ships. Plateau has grain, men, horses.

Who do you raid? Mughal frontier (rich, dangerous), Bijapur towns (rich, yours), Portuguese port (rich, guns).

Which foreign power? Portuguese (guns), Dutch (trade), British (contracts). Each alienates others.

When do you break away? Every fort you take, the Sultan notices. Too slow → never break. Too fast → crushed.

6. THE FIVE SYSTEMS
6.1 The Map (Overworld)
Fixed Deccan region

Settlements, forts, ports, roads, rivers, passes

Each location has a holder (you, another lord, the Sultan, foreign, independent)

Armies and parties move on this map

Time passes in days and seasons

6.2 Population & Economy
Villages have population, loyalty, prosperity

You assign population to roles: farmers, workers, artisans, soldiers

Buildings give roles and produce resources

5 resources: grain, timber, iron, cloth, coin

Demands: food (always), then cloth, then luxuries. Fulfilled = loyalty. Unfulfilled = unrest.

6.3 Army & Combat
3 unit types: infantry, cavalry, archers. Regional variants later.

Recruit from population. Training takes time.

Army has morale, wages, supply

Battles resolve on a tactical map (existing Game.js)

Terrain + weather matter: narrow passes, monsoon, hills

6.4 Politics & Relations
NPC lords with reputation, faction, goals

Actions: serve, ally, betray, marry, spy

The Sultan tracks your loyalty

Rebellion and secession are options, with consequences

6.5 The Living World
NPC lords act on their own. Some conquer, some develop, some raid.

Events fire based on world state: letters, betrayals, opportunities, crises.

The world continues whether you're watching or not.

That's it. Five systems. Each is numbers + state + UI.

7. THE POPULATION MODEL
This is the single most important design decision in the game.

7.1 The Model
Villages have people. You don't spawn them.

In 1600s India, a lord didn't conjure villagers. What a lord did:

Protect them → they stay, grow, pay tax, send recruits

Tax them fairly → they're loyal, productive

Tax them brutally → they flee, revolt, or die

Attract new ones → better conditions bring migrants

7.2 Population Growth/Shrink
Factor	Effect
Food surplus	+growth
Security (no raids)	+growth
Fair tax	+growth, +loyalty
Heavy tax	+coin now, −growth, −loyalty
Raided by enemy	−population, −loyalty
Famine (bad monsoon)	−population
Known as good lord	+migration
7.3 What You Build
You don't build houses to hold people. You build structures that give people roles:

Farm — food

Granary — stores surplus, prevents famine

Well — health, growth

Temple — loyalty, legitimacy

Market — trade, artisan jobs

Barracks — trains soldiers from population

Workshop — craftsmen, goods

Fort/Wall — security

Population grows into these structures. If you have 3 farms and 1 granary, you can support 200 people. Build 2 more farms and a market → support 400. Population grows over time.

7.4 Why This Is Right
Historically accurate. Lords inherited land with people. They didn't spawn citizens.

Simpler than Anno. No "build house, house fills, upgrade house."

More meaningful than AoE. Population is a relationship, not a number.

Sets up the reaction model. Every village is a mini-simulation.

8. THE NPC REACTION MODEL
No ML. No LLM. Rules + state + weighted randomness.

8.1 Why Not ML
Training a model requires: labeled data (which doesn't exist), months of training, integration into the game engine, and debugging via retraining. Total cost: 6–12 months for 10% improvement over rules.

Rule-based systems are: debuggable, tunable, fast, deterministic, understandable, and take 2 weeks.

8.2 Every NPC Lord Has
Relations with you (−100 to +100)

Ambition (1–10)

Loyalty to Sultan (0–100)

Fear of you (0–100)

Greed (1–10)

Memory of the last 10 actions you took toward them

8.3 Every Action You Take Updates These
Your Action	Effect
Gift 50 coin	+5 relations, +2 greed
Betray an ally	−30 relations with them, −10 with everyone
Win a battle	+10 fear from neighbors
Lose a battle	−10 fear, +5 ambition in rivals
Tax heavily	+2 coin/day, −3 loyalty/day, +1 fear
Marry into family	+20 relations, permanent alliance modifier
Spy and get caught	−40 relations, −15 reputation
Assassinate a lord	+5 fear, −20 reputation, blood feud chance
8.4 The Six Rules Every Lord Runs Daily
text
If relations < -50 and ambition > 6    → plot against you
If relations > +50 and fear < 30       → offer alliance
If fear > 70 and loyalty < 40          → submit or flee
If loyalty > 70 and Sultan weak        → scheme to dethrone
If you're weak                          → raid you
If you're strong                        → seek marriage/alliance
That's it. Six rules. Every NPC runs them. The result is a living world.

8.5 Weighted Randomness
Instead of deterministic checks, use weighted probability:

text
plotChance = (ambition * 0.1) + (negativeRelations * 0.01) - (fear * 0.02)
if random() < plotChance → begin plot
This produces emergent behavior that feels alive. Two lords with the same stats will react differently (random roll), but consistently with their personality (weights depend on stats).

This is the difference between "random" and "alive."

12. TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE
12.1 Stack
React 18 — UI layer (HUD, modals, menus)

Canvas 2D is enough for this game's scope. Hundreds of units, not thousands.

12.4 Pathfinding
A for units. Dijkstra for strategic layer queries.*

A* — unit movement, one-to-one, heuristic-guided. Keep existing.

Dijkstra — influence propagation, trade routes, threat mapping, recruitment reach. Cache results, recompute on world-state change.

Do not replace A*. Do not use BFS or Greedy Best-First.

When to upgrade: only if units exceed ~300 and frame time drops below 30fps. Then consider flow fields for groups. Not before.

12.5 Architectural Rules
The lord is an object from day 1, even if it's just a name and reputation. This allows the v2 character layer to be added without rewrite.

Relations are tracked from day 1, even if they're just numbers.

Battles are triggered from the overworld from day 1, even if they resolve simply.

No system should depend on a system that doesn't exist. Build the skeleton; add content; never add a new kind of system in v1.

13. THE GRAPHICS QUESTION
13.1 Current State
Procedural pixel art drawn in code with fillRect. Functional, readable, not beautiful.

13.2 The Ceiling
Realistic ceiling: Kingdoms and Castles / Rise to Ruins tier — systems-heavy 2D pixel strategy with functional art.

Not reachable solo: Rimworld / They Are Billions tier (needs a real artist), Northgard / Anno tier (studio-scale).

13.3 What AI Can/Cannot Do
Task	AI Alone	AI + You
Code	95%	100%
Pixel art (static)	30%	70% (with your cleanup)
Animation	5%	70% (procedural from static)
Music	60%	85% (curated)
Sound design	30%	70% (curated)
Feel / tuning	5%	95% (you playtest)
Level design	20–80% (genre)	90%
Game design taste	40% (sparring)	100% (you decide)
Long-term vision	0%	100%
13.4 The AI Sprite Workflow
AI generates base sprites (PixelLab, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney)

You set a fixed palette (16 colors max)

You recolor every sprite to match

You enforce fixed proportions (all humans same height)

You manually clean inconsistencies

Result: 60–70% AI, 30–40% you

Time cost: 60–100 hours for 200–400 sprites. Doable.

13.5 Animation
Keep procedural animation. Single sprite, moved and posed in code. This is what Dwarf Fortress, Prison Architect, and Rimworld do. Removes the animation bottleneck.

13.6 What "Premium" Actually Means
Not graphics horsepower. It means:

Consistency — same world, same tone

Restraint — no unnecessary effects

Timing — everything feels intentional

Sound — 50% of feel is audio

Readable UI — clean fonts, clear hierarchy

Polish on small things — button highlights, selection rings, placement animations

A clean-looking game with tight timing and good sound feels more premium than a gorgeous game with sloppy feedback. Every time.

Player role: Minor lord, not a named historical figure.

Population model: Villages with people, not spawned units.

Five systems: Map, Economy, Army, Politics, Living World.

Four screens: Overworld, Village, Army, Battle.

No ML. Rules + state + weighted randomness.

No multiplayer in v1.

Canvas 2D + React stack.

Battle uses existing Game.js engine.

Building list (currently ~20).

Unit types (currently 3).

16. RULES OF ENGAGEMENT FOR CONTRIBUTORS
For any new person (or AI agent) joining this project:

16.1 Before You Add Anything
Ask these five questions:

Does it change a decision the player makes? If no, it's decoration.

Can it be tested in one 30-minute playtest? If no, it's too big.

What does it cost in content, not code? Content is the real budget.

Does it survive on its own? If it only matters when combined with unbuilt systems, park it.

Is it a system or content? Content can be added anytime. Systems must be locked.

16.2 The Rule of Scope
The game is a year of work. Every new system adds a month. You do not have months.

If you're tempted to add a system, ask: "Can this be added in v2 without rewriting v1?" If yes, park it. If no, redesign.

16.3 The Rule of Commitment
Do not change direction for 6 months. The most common failure mode of this project is changing direction before shipping. Every pivot costs 3 months. Every feature added costs 1 month. Every abandoned direction costs everything.

16.4 The Rule of Building
Plan for a week. Build for a month. Then plan again.

Design documents have diminishing returns. After two weeks of planning, every additional week is a week not building. The skeleton is defined. Build it.

17. IMMEDIATE NEXT STEPS
17.1 The First Task
Build the overworld map.

Not the politics. Not the AI. Not the economy. The map.

Because the map is the thing that makes every other system concrete. Until you can:

See the Deccan region on screen

Move an army around it

Click on a village

See what it produces

...everything else is hypothetical.

17.2 The First Two Weeks
With AI's help, the following should exist by end of week 2:

A canvas rendering the Deccan map (150×100 cells)

5 regions visually distinct

8 villages, 3 towns, 4 forts, 1 port placed and labeled

Roads and rivers drawn

One movable army party

A day/night or season indicator

Basic HUD showing resources

That's the target. Two weeks. Then we build the next layer.

17.3 What To Do After
Week 3–4: Click a village, open a management panel, see population

Week 5–6: Assign roles, build one building, see production

Week 7–8: Add one NPC lord, one interaction (gift or raid)

Week 9–10: Trigger a battle, resolve using Game.js, return to map

Week 11–12: Add one event (a letter from the Sultan)

By week 12, you have a playable vertical slice. Not a game. A slice. But it proves the systems work together.

17.4 What To Do If Stuck
If a system feels too big: cut it in half. Ship the half.

If a feature is unclear: skip it. Add it in v2.

If you're tempted to change direction: don't. Finish the current task.

If you're bored: that's the signal to build, not plan.

If you're stuck on art: use procedural sprites. Ship the mechanics first.

19. FINAL THOUGHTS
19.1 The Vision in Three Sentences
A 2D pixel strategy game set in the Deccan, 1600s, where you live a life as a minor lord.

You grow your village, manage your people, build your army, and navigate politics through simple math that produces emergent, alive-feeling behavior.

The game ships in ~1 year with AI writing the code, and grows into a full Bannerlord-style sandbox over multiple versions — without ever rewriting v1.

19.2 The One Rule
Do not change direction for 6 months. Build the skeleton. Ship the vertical slice. Then decide what's next.

Every previous design conversation that never resulted in a shipped game failed for the same reason: the designer changed direction before finishing. This document exists so that doesn't happen again.

19.3 The Promise to the Player
You will not play a game about winning battles. You will play a game about living a life in a world that notices what you do, and reacts. You will not always win. You will sometimes lose everything. You will sometimes become someone you didn't expect. And that is the point.
